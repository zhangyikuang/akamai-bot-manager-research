> In August 2026, Akamai Bot Manager rolled out a refreshed build. This article documents how I reverse engineered its two-stage sensor challenge in about two weeks: the wrong turns, the methodology that survived build rotation, and why protocol shape mattered as much as the algorithm. Methodology only—no runnable bypass or live-session data is included.

## Why I Wrote This

Most public material on Akamai stops at emulating a browser environment to generate `sensor_data`. The updated system was more involved: the short report no longer determined the outcome. The decisive checks had moved to a much larger report with a completely different structure.

This article follows the investigation from an unknown system to a working result. The focus is not a list of answers—field names rotate between builds, so such answers expire—but the evidence behind each decision, including three major wrong turns and how I identified them.

## 1. Locating the Gate

The target was a GraphQL endpoint. It used no signature header, returned HTTP 403 to a bare request, and placed its entire gate in cookies. Searching for cookie values in DevTools and tracing their origin showed that `_abck` and the `bm_*` family were driven by several large scripts served from randomized paths.

The problem could therefore be reduced to one objective: **run those scripts locally and reproduce the reports generated by a real browser**.

## 2. The First Lesson in Stack Tracing: I Started in the Wrong Place

After neutralizing an infinite `debugger` loop—identified through a telemetry variable inside `eval`—I followed a core script toward its worker-message path and spent half a day analyzing the dispatcher:

![The worker-message path that turned out to be unrelated](images/01-worker-message-check.png)

That path never reached the target request. It belonged to the site's own telemetry system. The correction was simple: **start at the first stack frame of the target POST and trace backward from there**.

![The first stack frame of the target request](images/04-first-stack-ehwb.png)

I moved upward through the stack and inspected local variables at every frame. The plaintext assembly point eventually exposed an 82-element key/value array in Scope—the first-stage `sensor_data` payload:

![Tracing into the plaintext assembly point](images/08-stack-step2.png)

This lesson held throughout the project: when reversing heavily obfuscated code, choosing the right starting point often matters more than raw analysis speed.

## 3. Stage One: The Traditional `sensor_data` Route

Once the plaintext was visible, I followed the conventional approach: moved the script into a Node.js VM, implemented a minimal browser environment, and compared the local plaintext with a browser capture field by field.

Two details were especially useful:

- **Exception text becomes part of the payload.** When a Web API is missing, the script may encode the resulting `TypeError` instead of skipping the check. These differences deserve the highest priority because they expose an invalid environment directly.
- **Timing fields require a clean control run.** Several values initially differed by a factor of 30. After removing breakpoints and recapturing the browser run, they matched Node exactly. The debugger had altered the observation; the environment was not at fault.

Most difficulties in this stage were not algorithmic. They came from observing the system incorrectly—a theme that continued throughout the investigation.

## 4. `_abck` Remained at `-1`

After the first-stage environment was aligned, the short-report POST returned HTTP 201, but `_abck` remained in its `~-1~` state and the business endpoint still returned 403.

A frame-by-frame review of the HAR revealed a **second script** with a different URL shape. It submitted a `{"body":"..."}` payload, and one cookie moved into a usable state only after that long report was accepted. The conclusion was clear: **the short report registered the client; the decisive validation happened in the second stage**.

This was the first major turning point. The traditional route had solved only one part of the system.

## 5. The Counterexample That Narrowed the Problem

The prevailing assumption was that the server required a complete set of related cookies. I tested the opposite: replaying a **single browser-captured `bm_s` cookie** against the business endpoint returned HTTP 200.

That one experiment established three facts:

1. Companion cookies such as `_abck` were not required by the business endpoint.
2. `bm_s` was the value actually being checked.
3. Changes in the length of `bm_s` were useful evidence that the server had updated its decision state, but no particular length was a stable success constant across builds. The real test was whether the resulting cookie passed the business endpoint.

The objective became much smaller: **make the long report pass server-side evaluation**.

## 6. The Two Long-Report Forms

The complete page lifecycle can produce two long reports:

- The first, `s="f"`, contains 120 environment signals and base fingerprints, with no mouse data.
- The second, `s="t"`, carries the `me` mouse trace and embeds the complete first report.

A later minimal-chain experiment established an important distinction: **the initial gate is already complete after the first `s="f"` report passes and its response cookies are applied**. The second report is not required for initial access. A browser continuing to report activity is not the same as the gate requiring every report. The production path can stop after the first report; the second remains useful for behavioral and full-lifecycle research.

### Tracing a Long Report from the Encrypted POST Back to Plaintext

Function names were not a reliable way into the long-report code. The dependable entry point was the request itself: intercept `{"body":"..."}` at XHR `send`, confirm the URL and payload, and enter the first challenge-script frame in the call stack.

![Intercepting the long-report body at XHR send](images/10-chal2-stack-1.png)

Inside the obfuscated `cJJ` frame, the local variable `gHJ` was already the outer plaintext object before encoding. This screenshot comes from the second report, so `ver`, `ke`, and `me` are visible. The first report follows the same assembly and encoding path, but uses `s="f"` and contains no `me`.

![Locating the plaintext gHJ object in the cJJ frame](images/11-chal2-stack-2.png)

Following the call into `wIJ` exposed `SzJ`, the assembly point where collected signals enter the outer payload. This is a useful location for capturing browser ground truth and running field-by-field diffs, but it is not the original source of each signal. Individual meanings still have to be traced back to their generator functions.

![Locating the SzJ signal assembly object inside wIJ](images/12-chal2-stack-3.png)

Two mechanisms were particularly interesting:

**The missing `me` field was initially a false lead.** I assumed the event handler was failing to record input. In reality, the first report was designed without a mouse trace. My observation tool exited after the first request, so it could never reveal the second.

**The second report has a gate of its own.** A polling function checks whether an event counter is greater than zero. In a static environment, no user events occur and the report never fires naturally. Reproducing it requires delivering events to the handlers registered by the script itself, allowing the original condition to pass instead of calling an internal function from outside.

## 7. Signal Alignment: The Method Matters More Than Field Names

This was the most labor-intensive part of the project. The 120 signals covered WebIDL prototype ownership, native getter shape, media queries, cross-origin isolation consistency, storage quotas, voice-list fingerprints, resource ledgers, and even hashes of raw DOM attributes.

The critical constraint was that **field names rotate between builds**. During this investigation alone, prefixes changed through `ag`, `Dj`, `if`, `bo`, `Op`, and `Au`. Memorizing a field map would not survive the next build. I instead developed a repeatable workflow:

```text
Intercept the field write → capture the runtime stack → locate the direct generator
→ extract string constants from the surrounding bytecode VM to form a hypothesis
→ run a controlled-variable matrix, changing one environmental condition at a time
→ reproduce the browser's actual WebIDL shape → run a full regression
```

Three cases stand out:

- **The missing 23 signals, from 97 to 120.** My first guess was an iframe lifecycle issue. It was wrong. The collector ran in a dynamically created SharedWorker sub-realm; one missing constructor caused realm creation to throw, and the script swallowed the exception. The fix was a complete child-realm lifecycle, not an iframe patch.
- **A field returning `-2`.** Node's VM exposed `SharedArrayBuffer` while `crossOriginIsolated` was false, a combination that does not occur in the corresponding browser context. The built-in had to be removed at VM bootstrap; a normal environment-layer override could not hide it correctly.
- **A field containing `undef#`.** The source was a read of a DOM node's `firstChild`. A browser's prototype getter returns `null` when there is no child; the emulation returned `undefined` because the getter did not exist. Adding four prototype getters eliminated the non-session discrepancies: 113 of 120 signals matched the browser byte for byte, while the remaining seven were expected session noise such as clocks and memory values.

![The field-by-field discrepancy list during signal alignment](images/13-remaining-diff.png)

This work also produced a broader lesson: **the observation tool can become part of the experiment**. An early prototype-pollution probe made several fields appear absent. Replacing it with source injection plus a `Function.prototype.toString` guard removed the artifacts. From then on, any stubborn mismatch was first treated as a possible measurement error and only then as an environment defect.

## 8. Rethinking Fingerprints: Control the Hash Input

The canvas, WebGL, and audio work became much simpler after one realization:

> I could not change the server's hash algorithm, but I could reproduce the browser API values supplied as its input. If the environment returned the target machine's real values, the script would calculate the same hash itself.

There was no need to render the fingerprint genuinely inside Node. I tested that alternative seriously: 210 parameter combinations through a cross-platform renderer produced zero matches. Font rendering differs at the glyph-pixel level and cannot be recovered by tuning a few parameters. That negative result supported captured-value fallback as the practical route.

One engineering detail was easy to underestimate: large base64 payloads can be corrupted by clipboards or chat tools while still looking superficially intact. PNG chunk CRC checks exposed the damage, after which all large fingerprint artifacts were transferred as files.

## 9. Behavioral Experiments on the Second Report

Although the second report is not required by the initial gate, reproducing it still requires more than a non-empty `me` value. Forty events emitted synchronously produce timestamp deltas of only 0–1 ms. A single request may look plausible, but repeated traces reveal an obvious machine pattern.

The final experiment dispatched events asynchronously, approximated the distribution created by 60 Hz frame coalescing, inserted randomized pauses, varied the trajectory on every run, and kept all coordinates inside the declared viewport. No individual signal demanded this design; it followed from a threat model in which the server can compare reports over time.

## 10. Orchestration: Protocol Shape Matters as Much as the Algorithm

Once the offline values matched, the remaining failures came from protocol shape rather than cryptography:

- The page script wrote an auxiliary cookie. Cookie assignment in the environment had to update one item at a time; replacing the whole cookie string broke the script's delete-then-write sequence.
- Both script stages share one `window`, cookie jar, and clock in the browser. The minimal path generates the first long report and applies the response state in the same VM. Continuing to the second report requires keeping that realm alive rather than starting a second stateless process.
- The final failures came from two HTTP details: the format of an identifier header and the browser's cross-origin `Referer` reduction. Correcting them changed the business endpoint to HTTP 200. The hardest anti-automation investigation in the chain ultimately ended on two headers.

## 11. Results

- Started from the August 2026 build and reached business data after roughly two weeks of research.
- Verified that the first `s="f"` long report alone is sufficient for the initial gate; the second report is not a production dependency.
- Matched 113 of 120 signals byte for byte; the other seven were expected per-session noise.
- Replayed the methodology against rotated builds: all high-risk checks continued to pass, showing that the implementation reproduced browser semantics instead of hard-coding one obfuscated build.
- Cold-start execution of the full research chain took approximately 4.5 seconds.
